# 3 First hockey skills

- URL: https://wiki.atlantissports.org/getting-started/new-player/first-hockey-skills
- Path: `getting-started/new-player/first-hockey-skills`

¶ First Hockey Skills

This is where you begin to feel like a hockey player. The focus is not advanced skill, but
building clean, repeatable habits that will support everything you learn later.

Core principle: Control first. Speed later.

¶ Holding the Stick Correctly

A proper grip improves control, strength on the puck, and keeps play clean.

Grip fundamentals:

 
Hold the handle tight into your palm.

 
Press your thumb firmly along the back-side (hook-side) of the handle in a clear “thumbs up” position.

 
The hook of the stick should be turned toward your body.

 
Keep your wrist strong and neutral, avoid excessive bending.

Quick check: If your hook is turned away from you, your grip is wrong.

Common mistakes:

 
Stick pointing away from you. 

 
Weak grip, losing the stick with contact. 

 
Weak thumb, excessive extension of the wrist. 

Think: Firm, strong hands.

¶ One-Handed Play

Underwater Hockey is played with one hand on the stick at all times.
This keeps the game fair, controlled, and safe.

What this means:

 
Your stick hand plays the puck.

 
Your free arm stabilizes and helps you move.

 
You must resist the urge to use both hands when reaching.

Why it matters:

 
Encourages better positioning.

 
Improves puck skill over time.

 
Prevents dangerous contact.

Rule: If you cannot reach it with one hand, move your body closer.

¶ Free Arm Usage

Your free arm is a powerful tool for balance, control, and propulsion.
Beginners often forget to use it intentionally.

Use your free arm to:

 
Guide your descent.

 
Stabilize in the cobra position.

 
Assist with turns.

 
Add small bursts of propulsion.

Safety note:

 
Never grab opponents.

 
Never pull yourself along the bottom.

 
Keep movements controlled around other players.

Simple cue: The free arm supports, it never wrestles.

¶ First Puck Contact

Your first objective is simply learning how the puck moves.
Clean contact is more important than powerful contact.

Starting technique:

 
Approach from behind or slightly to the side.

 
Place the stick gently against the puck.

 
Push, don’t slap.

 
Keep the puck within arm’s reach.

Goal: Short, controlled movements.

Avoid:

 
Hitting or slapping the puck.

 
Playing the puck with your glove.

Power becomes useful later. Control wins early.

¶ Protecting the Puck

Once you touch the puck, your next job is to keep it.
Protection is mostly about body positioning, not strength.

Basic protection strategy:

 
Place your body between the puck and the opponent.

 
Keep the puck slightly underneath or beside you.

 
Stay low and stable.

If pressure arrives:

 
Move the puck into open space.

 
Angle away from danger.

 
Release early rather than losing control.

Common mistake: Trying to fight through multiple opponents.

Better choice: Protect → move → support.

¶ Looking Up While Handling

Awareness separates beginners from developing players.
You cannot make good decisions if your eyes are locked on the puck.

Build the habit early:

 
Glance at the puck.

 
Lift your eyes to scan.

 
Return focus briefly.

What to scan for:

 
Teammates.

 
Opponents.

 
Walls and boundaries.

 
Open lanes.

Simple cue: Handle → scan → decide.

At first this feels difficult. With repetition, it becomes automatic.

¶ Playing Along the Bottom

The bottom is your playing surface. Stability here allows better control and safer play.

Strong bottom habits:

 
Arrive balanced.

 
Adopt the cobra position when possible.

 
Keep your stick low.

 
Move with intention rather than scrambling.

Avoid:

 
Hips floating up.

 
Eyes facing down.

A stable player is a useful player.

¶ Basic Player Spacing

Good spacing prevents collisions and makes teamwork possible.

Beginner spacing rules:

 
Avoid clustering around the puck.

 
If a teammate is on the puck, create a nearby passing option.

 
Keep enough distance to react safely.

 
Do not swim directly over another player.

The beginner trap: Everyone chasing the puck.

The better habit: Support the play, don’t smother it.

Spread players create opportunities.
Crowded players create chaos.

Remember: These are your foundation skills. Precision now leads to confidence later.
Stay patient. Mastery is built through repetition.
